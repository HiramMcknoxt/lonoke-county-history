County topics

Narrative pages on the stories people ask about, each drawn from the named source and linked for the full telling. What the record doesn't settle, these pages say so.

The lone oak and the founding of the county

Lonoke is the rare county whose seat carries the same name — both taken from a single lone oak tree that surveyor George P. C. Rumbough used as a landmark while surveying for the Memphis and Little Rock Railroad. Governor Elisha Baxter signed the act creating the county on April 16, 1873, carving it from Prairie and Pulaski Counties.

Source: Encyclopedia of Arkansas.

Plum Bayou Mounds — the county's first landmark

Long before any town existed here, the people of the Plum Bayou culture built great ceremonial mounds near present-day Scott, roughly AD 650–1050. Gilbert Knapp, who owned the land in 1857, wrongly credited the Toltecs of Mexico — the name 'Toltec Mounds' stuck for more than a century. The site joined the National Register in 1973, became a National Historic Landmark in 1978, opened as a state park in 1980, and now carries its proper name: Plum Bayou Mounds Archeological State Park.

Source: Encyclopedia of Arkansas.

Brownsville, the Military Road, and the Civil War

Brownsville, on the federally funded Military Road, was the seat of Prairie County before Lonoke County existed — and a stage stop between DeValls Bluff and Little Rock. On August 25, 1863, some 6,000 Union cavalry under Brig. Gen. John W. Davidson fought Brig. Gen. John S. Marmaduke's 1,300 Confederates across Bayou Two Prairie; Union troops nearly destroyed Brownsville and Old Austin, sparing the Austin church for a hospital and the Brownsville courthouse. The town never recovered its standing.

Source: Encyclopedia of Arkansas.

Camp Nelson and Old Austin

Near Old Austin, the winter camp of Confederate Brig. Gen. Allison Nelson became one of Arkansas's few all-Confederate cemeteries: in 1862 some 1,500 soldiers died of disease there. A 20,000-pound, twelve-foot monument and 428 Arkansas marble headstones were dedicated on October 4, 1906. Camp Nelson is on the National Civil War Discovery Trail and joined the National Register in 1996. Old Austin itself — older than Cabot — keeps the antebellum Sears House and a Trail of Tears marker.

Source: Encyclopedia of Arkansas.

Eberts Field — Lonoke County trains WWI pilots

In 1917 Lonoke County outbid Pulaski County for a World War I aviation training field. Construction on Eberts Field began December 10, 1917, on Arkansas Highway 89 near Lonoke. The end of the war ended the field: it began closing in March 1919. A historic marker stands at the site today, and Signal Corps aerial photographs of the field survive in the photograph collection here.

Source: Encyclopedia of Arkansas.

Rice comes to the Grand Prairie

Cotton ruled Lonoke County until 1904, when W. H. Fuller proved rice could be produced profitably on the prairie's hardpan soil. Mills rose at Carlisle and Lonoke — the 1945 photograph of rice trucks filling downtown Carlisle is in the gallery — soybeans followed in 1940, and the prairie towns of Lonoke, Carlisle, and Humnoke still sit in rice-and-fish country.

Source: Encyclopedia of Arkansas.

The fish business — Joe Hogan and the minnow capital

The Joe Hogan Fish Hatchery, established at Lonoke in 1928, grew into a cornerstone of Arkansas aquaculture, and commercial baitfish farms around it — golden shiners, fathead minnows, goldfish — made the Lonoke area one of the nation's great minnow-producing regions. The gallery's hatchery photographs show the ponds, vats, harvest seines, and the 1930s hatchery truck that carried fish to lakes for stocking.

Source: Encyclopedia of Arkansas.

Railroad towns and strawberry season

The railroads built this county's towns and carried its crops: brokers bid on mule-drawn cotton wagons at the depots in Cabot, Carlisle, England, and Lonoke, and during strawberry season the Iron Mountain Railroad ran extra cars to carry berries from Cabot, Austin, and Ward. Depots still anchor the photograph collection — England's cotton bales in 1908, Carlisle's depot, Lonoke's Rock Island depot in 1973, Scott's depot, and Coy's abandoned depot in 1983.

Source: Encyclopedia of Arkansas.

Scott — plantations, Dortch, and the museum

Scott, straddling the Pulaski County line, holds the county's plantation landscape: the Dortch Plantation and its 1904 Marlsgate house, All Souls Church, Cotham's Mercantile, and — since its opening on June 25, 1989 — the Plantation Agriculture Museum and Scott Plantation Settlement, whose big house, shotgun tenant house, blacksmith shop, medical clinic, school house, and Seed Warehouse No. 5 preserve the working buildings of the cotton era.

Source: Encyclopedia of Arkansas.

Three governors from one county

Lonoke County has produced three Arkansas governors: James Philip Eagle of Lonoke, John Ellis Martineau, and Joseph Taylor Robinson — who also served ten years in the U.S. House and twenty-four in the U.S. Senate. Robinson's Lonoke home, decorated for his 1928 homecoming, is in the photograph gallery. Another native, Maurice Lee 'Footsie' Britt, served as lieutenant governor from 1967 to 1971, and Elsijane Trimble Roy became the first woman on the Arkansas Supreme Court.

Source: Encyclopedia of Arkansas.

Remington Arms and modern industry

Remington Arms is the largest manufacturer in Lonoke County today, joined by employers in Lonoke, Carlisle (Riviana, Stratton Seed), and Cabot (Dreamline). The plant's photograph in the gallery is dated May 2012; industry coverage in the county's newspapers is indexed on the Newspapers page.

Source: Encyclopedia of Arkansas.

The hard history — racial violence and lynching

An honest county archive carries this too. The Encyclopedia of Arkansas records multiple incidents of racial violence in late-19th- and early-20th-century Lonoke County: three members of the Eagle family killed in 1873, followed over the years by lynchings in 1882, 1892, 1894, 1910, and 1917, and a so-called race war in 1897. Lonoke County's lynching victims are memorialized at the National Memorial for Peace and Justice in Montgomery, Alabama — a photograph of that memorial is in the gallery, because remembering is part of the record.

Source: Encyclopedia of Arkansas.
